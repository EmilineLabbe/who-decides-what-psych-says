Who Decides What Psychology Says?


Public perception of typical human behavior is attributed to what psychology says. This common framing masks the debates and opinions underlying theories of human behavior stemming from individual researchers.

Knowledge as a source of truth

In the scientific process, data, obtained through experimentation, is analyzed to produce verifiable evidence for facts, from which truths are derived. Truth is limited by the data sampled and the perspective it is interpreted through, and is thereby always a product of time, place, and experience.

Psychology legitimizes normative structures

Psychology—the study of both observable behavior and the intangible mind—is a particularly personal subject, constrained by the experiential aspects. In every subject, researchers make choices about how to interpret data, but in psychology, findings define what is ‘normal’ and ‘right,’ canonizing how you should live. Documented patterns become reference points against which people are assessed. As the field of psychology progresses and integrates new, more rigorous methods—fMRI, eye-tracking, and EMA, to name a few—findings take on an increasingly objective tone, when they will always be, in some essence, snapshots filtered through the philosophies of individuals.

Researchers and subjects are WEIRD

Psychology’s subjects are disproportionately drawn from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) countries (Henrich et al., 2010). Sample diversification alone cannot resolve bias. A study may recruit participants from non-WEIRD countries, but final interpretation remains with the researchers themselves.

This source of subjectivity raises a question of who, from where, generates the findings that form established psychology? Recent work agrees that they, too, overwhelmingly come from WEIRD countries—2026 analyses report between 70 to 80 percent WEIRD-affiliated authorship in their samples (Džanko et al., 2026; Petrutiu et al., 2026). Homogeneity of cultural, educational, economic, financial, and political experience narrows the questions and opinions guiding normative frameworks. Findings are often generalized beyond the population studied, leading to labeling of non-WEIRD practices as abnormal (Nielsen et al., 2017).

"The present study"

Understanding which people from which places are contributing to psychology is critical for evaluating what ‘psychology says.’ This analysis relies on authorship data from publications indexed by Scopus, a leading abstract and indexing database of peer-reviewed literature overseen by Elsevier, one of the world’s largest publishers. Though Elsevier is based in the Netherlands, they require that indexed work have at minimum an abstract and title in English, and may not fully capture locally indexed or non-English research traditions. Global psychology research is partially isolated within regional databases, such as China’s National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Russia’s Russian Science Citation Index (RSCI). As a result of this bibliometric fragmentation, Scopus authorship patterns are best interpreted as representative of what English-speakers are engaging with.


Key findings

  1. The US contributes heavily to psychology: between 2011 and 2022, it produced nearly twice as much of the world's psychology (33.8%) as science (17.4%) with only 4.3% of the global population.
  2. China is the global leader in science, yet fifth in psychology researcher: between 2011 and 2022, it produced four times as much science (20.2%) as it did psychology (4.7%).
  3. Over half (58%) of psychology research comes from just five countries, four of which are WEIRD.
  4. WEIRD countries produced two-thirds of the world's psychology publications in 2022, down from 90% in 2003.

The US produced a third of the world's psychology publications

Scopus Psychology Publications by Country (2011-2022), Fractional Contributions


Between 2011 and 2022, 58% of the world's psychology publications came from 5 wealthy countries: the US, the UK, Germany, China, and Canada. US-affiliated authors contributed to a third of publications, roughly double its share of overall science (17%), with only 4 percent of the population.

The UK, Germany, and Canada contributed at a similar rate relative to their populations, though China did not: US contributions were 5 times that of China, who has a population over 4 times larger. Though it is expected that China contributes a smaller share given their own domestic database, their publications across all S&E topics is 1.4 times that of the US. In psychology specifically, China contributes less to Scopus, though this analysis cannot determine if that is database-specific or a larger pattern in their country's research portfolio.

Data and methods

  • Psychology publications: National Science Foundation Science & Engineering Indicators (Table SPBS-15, 2003–2022)
  • Denominator: National Science Foundation Science & Engineering Indicators (Table SPBS-2, 2003–2022)
  • Taiwan is not covered by World Bank data (non-UN member); population and GDP ($) are supplemented from Taiwan’s own national statistics agency (DGBAS)

Fractional counting

A fractional publication count attributes credit based on the proportion of authors affiliated with institutions in each country.

Example: If a publication has 4 authors (2 from the US, 1 from Canada, and 1 from the UK) each country receives:

  • US = 0.5 (2/4)
  • Canada = 0.25 (1/4)
  • UK = 0.25 (1/4)

This approach better reflects the collaborative nature of modern scientific research compared to whole counting (where each country gets full credit) or first-author counting (where only one country gets credit).
