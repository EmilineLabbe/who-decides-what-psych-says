Who Decides What Psychology Says?

Interpretation of human behavior depends on who writes it.

As the field of psychology progresses and integrates new, more replicable methods, findings take on an increasingly objective tone. Yet psychology is a particularly personal subject. A researcher's perspective, grounded in lived experience, guides interpretation of the behaviors they study and the work they publish.

Even the most evidence-based scientific truth is limited by study samples and the worldviews through which data is understood. The impact is amplified in psychology: results that document patterns of behavior end up defining how people are supposed to behave (Cheon et al., 2020; Nielsen et al., 2017), despite these findings emerging from limited populations studied within specific contexts (Rad et al., 2018).

Identifying who, from where, is publishing research is critical for evaluating psychological claims. One approach to reducing bias is sample diversification, but simply widening the pool cannot resolve the problem on its own: final interpretation remains with those who publish. These people often work in Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) countries (Henrich et al., 2010). Between 2020 and 2023, over 70 percent of psychology authors were WEIRD-affiliated (Džanko et al., 2026). Limited representation breeds homogeneity of lived experience, narrowing the questions and assumptions guiding normative frameworks.

Cross-national analyses using the National Science Foundation's publication counts documented in its Science & Engineering (NSF S&E) Indicators maps where psychology is written and how international presence is changing.

Key findings

Figures and findings cover 2011–2022 unless noted.

  1. Among large science producers, psychology's share is higher in the WEIRD ones. Every WEIRD country in the top-15 producers of science devotes at least 2.2% of its output to psychology and every non-WEIRD country devotes 1.3% or less. The split is between North American/European countries and South American/Asian countries.
  2. The US writes more psychology than any other country. It writes a third of psychology publications (33.8%), nearly double its share of science and roughly eight times its fraction of the world's population.
  3. Psychology can be a small share of a country's science and still account for a large share of the world's psychology. Psychology is 0.45% of China's science output, but volume is still substantial given its massive scale of scientific output. China is the only non-WEIRD member of the top five in cumulative output, and by 2021 surpassed the UK to claim second-place in annual output.
  4. Psychology's center of production is shifting away from the US. The US share of psychology has nearly halved since 2003. It fell from 46.8% to 27.7% of annual world psychology output, while China's rose from 0.7% to 11.1%.

Whether this reflects a top-down national push for psychology research or individual researcher inclinations shaped by culture is uncertain.

What is clear is that among the top-producers, psychology occupies more of the research system in Western countries. From this, representation becomes a two-part question: volume shows how much of the field a country writes, but share shows how much of that country's research system does the writing.

The US writes more psychology than any other country.

The US wrote a third of the world's psychology across the period, 33.8%, nearly double its 17.4% of world science. With 4.3% of the global population, that is roughly eight times its population share.

The concentration of psychology publications extends beyond the US, pooling in five top producers who wrote 58% of the field. Four of them are WEIRD.

Five wealthy countries produced 58% of the world's psychology publications, ranked from highest to lowest: the US, the UK, Germany, Canada, and China.

Most of sub-Saharan Africa and Central Asia contributed a few hundred publications each across the full twelve years, against the US's 201,665.

Psychology can be a small share of a country's science and still account for a large share of the world's psychology.

China's science base is 1.2 times the US base, yet its cumulative psychology output ranks fifth globally. Similarly, the UK and Japan produce nearly identical volumes of science, yet the UK produces roughly four times Japan's psychology. Composition explains the divergence within these pairs.

Scopus' English-language requirement and China's domestic database (CNKI) may account for part of China's gap, though its lead in total science suggests other subjects are contributed at higher rates to global databases compared to psychology.

The contributor base is diversifying by volume. Whether it is diversifying by share is a different question. China's rise runs through the smallest research-system share of any top-five producer; no other sits below 2.9%.

What this all means

Psychology's contributor base is broader than it was twenty years ago, yet narrower than it looks on the surface.

  1. International volume is becoming less WEIRD: the US share nearly halved, and China now ranks second in annual output.
  2. Intranational share remains divided by Western-affiliation: psychology still claims 2-4% of every top WEIRD research system and 1.3% or less of every non-WEIRD one, and nearly half the WEIRD decline runs through a country that devotes 0.45% of its system to the field.

For now, what psychology says is still written overwhelmingly inside Western research systems.

Quantifying publication counts

Coverage

Scopus is the world's leading abstract and citation database run by Elsevier, one of the world’s largest publishers. Indexed content must be peer-reviewed and have a title and abstract available in English.

Fractional contributions

NSF aggregates fractional publication counts by country. A fractional publication count attributes credit based on the proportion of authors on a single paper affiliated with institutions in each country.

Example: If a publication has 4 authors (2 from the US, 1 from Canada, and 1 from the UK) each country receives:

  • US = 0.5 (2/4)
  • Canada = 0.25 (1/4)
  • UK = 0.25 (1/4)

This approach better reflects the collaborative nature of modern scientific research compared to whole counting (where each country gets full credit) or first-author counting (where only one country gets credit).


Defining WEIRD Countries

Russia is coded as non-WEIRD; US-developed system justification scales fail on Russian samples without substantial rewriting (Agadullina et al., 2021).

Limitations

  1. Elsevier requires that work indexed by Scopus has at minimum an abstract and title in English, and cannot fully capture non-English research cultures. The complete global body of psychology research is dispersed across global and regional databases, most notably China’s National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Russia’s Science Citation Index (RSCI). Scopus authorship patterns are best interpreted as representative of what English-speakers are engaging with.
  2. Institutional affiliation includes researchers imported from non-WEIRD countries. Aggregate counts may reflect globalized perspectives, particularly in highly internationalized research hubs (e.g. the US).
  3. Fractional counting gives equal weight all individual contributors and does not consider first/last author status. This assumes that on average, all listed authors meaningfully influence published perspectives to the same degree.
  4. Comparing psychology output to overall scientific output only maps the global landscape. A follow-up comparing psychology to other subjects is needed to isolate how different countries handle psychology research in particular.
