Who Decides What Psychology Says?

Public understanding of typical human behavior is shaped by the perception of psychology as an authoritative voice.

As the field of psychology progresses and integrates new, more replicable methods, findings take on an increasingly objective tone. Yet psychology is a particularly personal subject. A researcher's worldview, grounded in lived experience, guides their interpretation of the actions of those they observe. The mind studies itself.

The process of discovery is constructive. In science, data, obtained through experimentation, is analyzed to produce verifiable evidence for facts, which contribute to understandings of truth. Understandings of scientific truth are shaped by time, place, and experience, inherently limited by the data sampled and the perspectives through which they are interpreted. The impact is amplified in psychology: results that document patterns of behavior end up defining how people are supposed to behave, though these findings emerge from limited populations studied within specific contexts.

Identifying who, from where, is publishing research is critical for evaluating psychology research. Researchers overwhelmingly come from from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) countries (Henrich et al., 2010), at rates of 70 to 80 percent WEIRD-affiliated authorship (Džanko et al., 2026; Petrutiu et al., 2026). Homogeneity of cultural, educational, economic, financial, and political experience narrows the questions and opinions guiding normative frameworks.

Publication counts aggregated in National Science Foundation Science & Engineering (NSF S&E) Indicators reflect the current state of affairs, including which countries produce science and how much of it is psychology.

Key findings

  1. The US leads the world in psychology. It writes a third of the world's psychology (33.8%), nearly double its share of science (17.4%). Holding only 4.3% of the global population, it is highly overrepresented.
  2. China leads the world in science, yet trails the US, the UK, Canada, and Germany in psychology. It is the only non-WEIRD member of the top-5 contributors to global psychology.
  3. The top 15 largest science producers produce psychology research at varying rates. Psychology share of national output spans 0.3% (India) to 4% (the UK). The US ranks third at 3.8%, publishing psychology at a high rate on top of an already large research base.
  4. The contributor landscape is diversifying. WEIRD countries produced two-thirds of the world's psychology publications in 2022, down from 90% in 2003. The US annual share fell from 46.8% to 27.7% during this time, while China's rose from 0.7% to 11.1%.

The UK, Germany, and Canada also contributed similar rates relative to their populations, though China did not: US contributions were 5 times that of China, who has a population over 4 times larger. Though it is expected that China contributes a smaller share given their own domestic database, their publications across all S&E topics is 1.2 times that of the US. In psychology specifically, China contributes less to Scopus, though this analysis cannot determine if that is database-specific or a larger pattern in their country's research portfolio.

Data and methods

  • Publication counts are sourced from the National Science Foundation (NSF) Science & Engineering (S&E) Indicators, 2003–2022 (Table SPBS-2, all S&E articles; SPBS-15, psychology S&E articles).
  • GDP per capita ($) is retrieved from the World Bank's World Development Indicator (WDI) database.
    • Taiwan is not covered by World Bank data (non-UN member); population and GDP ($) are supplemented from Taiwan’s Directorate General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics (DGBAS) Macro Database, which acts as a census bureau.
  • Full analysis, including additional regression diagnostics: notebooks/01_data_insights.ipynb.

Quantifying publication counts

Coverage

Scopus is the world's leading abstract and citation database run by Elsevier, one of the world’s largest publishers. Indexed content must be peer-reviewed and have a title and abstract available in English.

Fractional contributions

NSF aggregates fractional publication counts by country. A fractional publication count attributes credit based on the proportion of authors on a single paper affiliated with institutions in each country.

Example: If a publication has 4 authors (2 from the US, 1 from Canada, and 1 from the UK) each country receives:

  • US = 0.5 (2/4)
  • Canada = 0.25 (1/4)
  • UK = 0.25 (1/4)

This approach better reflects the collaborative nature of modern scientific research compared to whole counting (where each country gets full credit) or first-author counting (where only one country gets credit).

Limitations

  1. Elsevier requires that work indexed by Scopus has at minimum an abstract and title in English, and cannot fully capture non-English research cultures. The complete global body of psychology research is dispersed across global and regional databases, most notably China’s National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Russia’s Science Citation Index (RSCI). Scopus authorship patterns are best interpreted as representative of what English-speakers are engaging with.
  2. Institutional affiliation includes researchers imported from non-WEIRD countries. Aggregate counts may reflect globalized perspectives, particularly in highly internationalized research hubs (e.g. the US).
  3. Fractional counting gives equal weight all individual contributors and does not consider first/last author status. This assumes that on average, all listed authors meaningfully influence published perspectives to the same degree.
